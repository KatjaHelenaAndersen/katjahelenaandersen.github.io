## Work in Progress

### It’s Not Just the Diagnosis: Job Skills, Health Shocks and Mental Health
*Work in progress*

Using a combination of administrative records on healthcare utilisation and detailed skill requirement data, I study how health shocks that hinder employment affect workers’ mental health. A health shock can harm mental health directly, but also by threatening a worker’s ability to keep doing their job. I classify occupations by their reliance on five skill domains, such as physical strength, fine motor skills and vision, and classify ICD-10 diagnoses, including injuries, accidents, cancers and other diseases, by which of these domains they impair. This allows me to compare workers who receive the same diagnosis within a short interval, but whose jobs differ in whether they require the affected skill. Because both groups experience the same health shock, the comparison addresses selection into the shock and isolates the employment-hindering component. I estimate effects in a staggered difference-in-differences design on mental health outcomes, including prescriptions for antidepressants and anxiolytics and outpatient contacts with psychologists, psychiatrists and talk therapy.

### Who Killed the Academic Dream? Governance and Mental Health
*With Seetha Menon. Work in progress*

We study how a shift from collegial to managerial governance affects the mental health of university employees. We exploit the 2003 Danish University Act, which replaced elected collegial bodies with appointed leadership and board-based governance at all public universities in a country long known for extensive academic self-governance. Using administrative records on healthcare utilisation, we find no robust aggregate effect on mental health care use. Comparisons within universities, however, reveal diverging responses. Non-academic staff increased their use of psychiatric medication relative to academics, particularly women and employees without prior treatment. Among academics, junior women reduced their use of psychiatric care relative to senior women, a result that persists when accounting for exits from university employment. Staff at a university that reformed earlier showed no response, consistent with a smooth transition there. Our paper provides some of the first causal evidence on how managerial governance affects employee mental health.

### When the Safety Net Shrinks: Unemployment Insurance Generosity and Mental Health
*With Seetha Menon and Giovanni Mellace. Work in progress*

Unemployment insurance (UI) may protect mental health before any claim is made, by limiting what a layoff would cost workers who fear one, especially in a recession. The 2008 to 2010 crisis made jobs less secure: full-time employment fell by about a fifth in construction and manufacturing and grew in public administration, education and health. During this crisis, Denmark announced a cut in maximum UI duration from four to two years. Using population-wide registers, we study whether the cut worsened the mental health of employed workers who feared a layoff, and whether a benefit to fall back on after UI softened the effect. To our knowledge, no study has examined the cut’s effect on employed workers’ mental health.
